# Booking Right, Getting Scammed Anyway: A Car Rental Cautionary Tale from Southern Italy

For more than fifteen years I've booked rental cars through [https://www.autoeurope.com](AutoEurope), even before moving back to Italy. It's a broker that lets you compare providers and, crucially, lets you pre-pay for the "excess waiver" (*franchigia* in Italian) the deductible you'd otherwise owe out of pocket (sometimes 500 euros or more) if the car is damaged, has its windows broken, or is stolen, even when it's not your fault. Over the years this has consistently worked well for me.

This summer, renting a car in southern Italy through a local agency, that streak hit two rough patches. A quick note on the setup: I booked and paid for the rental remotely, in my own name, but I wasn't the one on-site. The person actually collecting and driving the car was an elderly family member of mine. That's relevant to what follows.

## The waiver dispute

At the counter of the local rental agency, despite the waiver from AutoEurope was clearly printed on the voucher to handle the company, the agent insisted no waiver was included, claiming maybe that would only applied "with AutoEurope," not with them, and that whoever was picking up the car would have to pay the deductible upfront and seek possibly reimbursement later. Because I'd handled the booking remotely, the agency called me directly, on the phone, while my family member stood at the counter, to try to talk me into buying the waiver (again!) or into extra add-ons for her. I politely pushed back and refused, pointing out it was already on the voucher. AutoEurope, when I complained mid-rental, said they couldn't open a formal ticket until the rental was complete. I'm now filing that formal complaint.

## The fuel charge

The second incident was worse. Somewhere along the way, without a clear conversation about it, a 70-euro "full tank" charge was applied to the booking, and the agency's own paperwork dates it to the very first day of the rental, not the return. The car was returned full, as agreed and as shown on the digital fuel gauge, so the charge made no sense either way. It appears on a signed digital tablet printout, but my family member doesn't recall agreeing to it. I have no proof of exactly how or when it was added to the agreement, but the amount and the way it surfaced are suspicious enough that I'm flagging it here as a pattern, not a proven fraud.

## Three things I now always do

**Photograph the whole car at pickup, immediately, and send the photos to yourself or the agency on the spot** — not just leaving them sitting in your phone's camera roll. That way there's a timestamped, independent record if they later claim a scratch that wasn't already there.

**If returning with a full tank, refill twenty to thirty minutes before drop-off** at a nearby station rather than right at the door, and keep both the receipt and a photo of it. In Italy specifically, I've had agencies dispute a full tank hours or days after return, even when the digital gauge clearly read full. A receipt makes that dispute much harder for them to make.

**Don't assume a paid-for service protects you just because it's written down.** Keep the voucher accessible, and if there's a dispute, say so clearly and immediately, ideally in front of a witness.

## A last thought

I understand the economic pressure independent rental agencies are under, and that upselling insurance and add-ons is how many survive in a competitive market. That's fair enough. What's harder to accept is a pattern where the elderly, or anyone who seems less likely to push back, gets targeted more aggressively than everyone else. In a country where the extended family is such a central value, that shouldn't be a blind spot. The same care we're expected to show our own relatives is worth extending to someone else's, too. I don't think this is high philosophy for a post about renting cars, but it's worth saying anyway.
